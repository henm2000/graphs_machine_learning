\begin{abstract}
Positional encodings are the standard remedy for the 1-Weisfeiler--Leman bound on
message-passing networks, and the usual question is which signal to compute. We report that how
it is injected matters more. Our vehicle is a traversal-based \emph{relative} encoding: the
sinusoidally encoded hop distance $d(u,v)$ on every node pair within $r$ hops, anchor-free where
absolute traversal encodings are not, and needing rewiring to reach a sparse architecture at
all. On CLUSTER the identical signal is worth $+21.3$ points under GAT and nothing under GCN
($-0.9$). That cell is three seeds, its gain is under $2\sigma$ of the control's seed spread and
its ablations are GCN-only, so we read it as a direction rather than a measurement. A
proposition explains the asymmetry: since GCN and GAT both \emph{normalise} their aggregation,
an edge-weight or attention-bias injection cancels algebraically when weighted degree is
constant and features uniform, so on CSL such a model provably cannot beat chance, and does
not. We also place the encoding in the hierarchy,
$\text{1-WL}\subsetneq\textsc{t-rpe}\subseteq\text{GD-WL}\subseteq\text{3-WL}$, on explicit
witnesses, and report that our pre-registered sparse-versus-dense prediction failed in the
opposite direction.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Message-passing graph neural networks (MPNNs) aggregate information over local neighbourhoods,
and their ability to distinguish graphs is bounded by the 1-Weisfeiler--Leman colour refinement
test \citep{xu2019gin,morris2019wlgo}. The bound is not a technicality: an MPNN cannot see
global symmetry, measure how far apart two nodes are, or separate graphs that agree locally but
differ globally.

Positional encodings (PEs) are the standard response. They enrich node features with a
structural signal computed outside the message-passing recursion, and they come in two
families. \emph{Absolute} PEs assign a vector to each node, Laplacian eigenvectors
\citep{dwivedi2023benchmarking,dwivedi2021generalization}, random-walk return probabilities
\citep{dwivedi2022lspe}, or distances to a set of anchors
\citep{you2019pgnn,li2020distance}. \emph{Relative} PEs assign a value to each node
\emph{pair}, and are the norm in graph transformers, where a pairwise bias is added to every
attention logit \citep{ying2021graphormer,rampasek2022gps}.

Traversal-based encodings have so far lived almost entirely in the absolute family: a
breadth-first search from an anchor produces a per-node distance that drops straight into the
feature vector, but the encoding is then a function of the anchor set, and that set falls to a
heuristic whose ties are broken arbitrarily. The relative formulation has no anchors: $d(u,v)$ is
a property of the pair.

The obstacle is that a relative encoding has nowhere to live in a sparse MPNN, since every edge
joins nodes at distance exactly one and a function of $d(u,v)$ restricted to $E$ is constant.
Making it meaningful requires changing which pairs exchange messages: rewiring
\citep{bruel2022rewiring,topping2022curvature}. We take the natural construction, an edge for
every pair within $r$ hops carrying its hop distance. Rewiring is not neutral --- it relieves
over-squashing \citep{alon2021bottleneck} but multiplies the edge count --- so a plain baseline
confounds the encoding with the rewiring it requires, and we run two controls: the rewired graph
with a \emph{constant} edge feature, and the unrewired graph at $r=1$.

Our contributions:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item \textbf{Injection decides the outcome}, in theory and in measurement. Because GCN and GAT
  both \emph{normalise} their aggregation, an edge-weight or attention-bias injection cancels
  algebraically once weighted degree is constant and features uniform
  (Proposition~\ref{prop:cancel}), so on CSL it cannot help however expressive the encoding is,
  and measurably does not; outside that setting the same signal is worth $+21.3$ points under GAT
  and $-0.9$ under GCN (\S6). Prior work reports relative encodings almost only inside dense
  attention, where the issue never surfaces.
\item \textbf{A relative traversal encoding} (\TRPE) as the vehicle (\S\ref{sec:method}), placed
  in the expressiveness hierarchy (\S\ref{sec:theory}) on explicit witnesses: above 1-WL,
  strengthened by shortest-path counts, below 3-WL, blind to strongly regular graphs.
\item \textbf{An evaluation designed around the mechanism} (\S\ref{sec:setup}), contrasting a
  sparse long-diameter benchmark with a dense short-diameter one, on a prediction registered
  before training.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Absolute positional encodings.}
\citet{dwivedi2023benchmarking} introduced Laplacian eigenvector PEs, a global spectral
coordinate system: expressive, but $\mathcal{O}(|V|^3)$ to decompose and defined only up to a
per-eigenvector sign \citep{lim2023signnet}. Random-walk PEs \citep{dwivedi2022lspe} record
$k$-step return probabilities, cheaper and sign-free. \citet{you2019pgnn} and
\citet{li2020distance} use distances to sampled or selected anchors, the closest prior work in
signal but not in form: per-node and anchor-dependent.

\paragraph{Relative positional encodings.}
Relative position is standard in sequence models \citep{vaswani2017attention}, and the graph
analogue lives in graph transformers: Graphormer
\citep{ying2021graphormer} biases every attention logit by shortest-path distance and GraphGPS
\citep{rampasek2022gps} unifies such biases with local message passing, both paying
$\mathcal{O}(|V|^2)$ for dense attention. Our question is whether the relative view survives in
a \emph{sparse} architecture, where the bias must ride on explicitly added edges.

\paragraph{Rewiring and expressiveness.}
\citet{alon2021bottleneck} identified over-squashing and \citet{topping2022curvature} tied it to
negatively curved edges, motivating schemes that add or reweight edges. Closest to us,
\citet{bruel2022rewiring} rewire using positional information and feed the encoding as an edge
attribute; our construction follows theirs, and we add the traversal signal, the expressiveness
analysis, and a control separating rewiring from encoding. On expressiveness,
\citet{xu2019gin} and \citet{morris2019wlgo} established the 1-WL bound and
\citet{zhang2023biconnectivity} define generalised-distance WL (GD-WL), showing shortest-path
distance already yields a strictly stronger test. We propose no new variant, only place ours.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Traversal.}
For a graph $G=(V,E)$ and a radius $r$, a breadth-first traversal from each $u\in V$ to depth $r$
yields the hop distance $d(u,v)$ for every $v$ within $r$ hops, and, at no extra cost, the
number $\#\mathrm{sp}(u,v)$ of shortest paths realising it, at
$\mathcal{O}(|V|(|V|+|E|))$ per graph, computed once. In practice we take both from $r$ matrix
products instead: the smallest $k$ with $(A^k)_{uv}>0$ is $d(u,v)$, and since walks of that
length are exactly shortest paths, $(A^{d(u,v)})_{uv}=\#\mathrm{sp}(u,v)$. We verify this against
a reference breadth-first search.

\paragraph{Rewiring and encoding.}
We take $E_r=\{(u,v):1\le d(u,v)\le r\}$; at $r=1$ this is the original graph and the encoding is
constant, making $r=1$ a built-in degenerate control rather than only a hyperparameter setting.
Each rewired edge carries $\gamma(d(u,v))\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$, a sinusoidal embedding of its hop
distance in the sense of \citet{vaswani2017attention}, so nearby distances receive nearby
representations, and a small MLP maps $\gamma(d)$ --- optionally concatenated with
$\log(1+\#\mathrm{sp}(u,v))$ --- to the edge representation $e_{uv}$.

\paragraph{Injection.}
The encoding enters through two channels. The first is the natural one for each architecture: in
GAT, $e_{uv}$ is an additive bias on the attention logit of $(u,v)$, which is what a relative
positional encoding means in the transformer sense; in GCN, which has no edge-feature channel,
$e_{uv}$ becomes a learned scalar gate $w_{uv}=\sigma(\mathbf{w}^\top e_{uv})$ used as an edge
weight. The second is not optional \emph{on vertex-transitive or uniform-feature graphs}, for
reasons Proposition~\ref{prop:cancel} makes precise, though \S6 shows it is not universally
beneficial either: each node also receives its aggregated relative-distance profile,
\begin{gather*}
p_v=\!\!\sum_{u\,:\,1\le d(u,v)\le r}\!\!\gamma\big(d(u,v)\big),\\
x_v\leftarrow\big[\,x_v\ \|\ \mathrm{MLP}(p_v)\,\big],
\end{gather*}
which is anchor-free, permutation invariant, and exactly the aggregation GD-WL performs. For the
$\#\mathrm{sp}$ variant the summand is $[\gamma(d),\ \log(1+\#\mathrm{sp})\cdot\gamma(d)]$,
keeping distance and path count \emph{jointly}: summing them separately would discard which path
counts belong to which distance. Since $d$ and $\#\mathrm{sp}$ are isomorphism invariants of the
pair, both channels are permutation equivariant and the pooled output invariant, which we verify
empirically; the obvious alternative is not, a BFS \emph{visit-order} index changing under
relabelling.

\section{Expressive Power}
\label{sec:theory}

Write $c^t(v)$ for the colour of $v$ after $t$ refinement rounds. \WL{} refines by
$c^{t+1}(v)=\mathrm{HASH}(c^t(v),\{\!\{c^t(u):u\in N(v)\}\!\})$. Define \SPD{} by
\begin{multline*}
c^{t+1}(v)=\\
\mathrm{HASH}\big(c^t(v),\{\!\{(d(u,v),c^t(u)):u\neq v\}\!\}\big),
\end{multline*}
with $d(u,v)=\infty$, a distinguished symbol distinct from every integer, when $u$ and $v$ lie in
different components, and \SPC{} identically but with the pair label
$(d(u,v),\#\mathrm{sp}(u,v))$. For $r\ge\mathrm{diam}(G)$, \TRPE{} realises \SPD{}; the M4
variant realises \SPC{}. For $r<\mathrm{diam}(G)$ it realises a different, weaker test in which
every pair beyond $r$ carries the label $\infty$; every experimental claim below except CSL, run
at $r=6=\mathrm{diam}$, uses that truncated version.

\begin{proposition}[Lower bound]\label{prop:lower}
\SPD{} refines \WL{}, strictly.
\end{proposition}
Refinement is immediate: the neighbours of $v$ are exactly the pairs with $d(u,v)=1$, so any
\WL{} class is a union of \SPD{} classes. For strictness, decalin versus bicyclopentyl serves
($10$ nodes, $11$ edges, same degree sequence), and CSL gives a second witness the experiments
exercise directly.

\begin{proposition}[Ceiling on strongly regular graphs]\label{prop:upper}
Neither \SPD{} nor \SPC{} separates two \emph{connected} strongly regular graphs with the same
parameters.
\end{proposition}
Strongly regular graphs with equal parameters have identical distance distributions by
definition: adjacent pairs share $\lambda$ common neighbours, non-adjacent pairs $\mu$, and
$\mu>0$ (connectedness) forces diameter $2$, so every pair carries the same label under either
test. The $4\times4$ rook's graph and the Shrikhande graph are both $\mathrm{SR}(16,6,2,2)$, and
neither separates them.

Against 3-WL, containment holds. 2-FWL (3-WL in the usual GNN naming) refines a colouring of
\emph{pairs} and can compute matrix products over it, so it obtains every $(A^k)_{uv}$, hence
$d(u,v)$ as the smallest $k$ with $(A^k)_{uv}>0$ and $\#\mathrm{sp}(u,v)$ as its value there:
both pair labels are functions of the 3-WL colouring, giving $\SPD,\SPC\subseteq\text{3-WL}$.
What we leave open is \emph{strictness}, which the rook/Shrikhande pair cannot witness, since
3-WL is stable at the initial colouring there too --- the refinement multiset of a pair depends
only on $(k,\lambda,\mu)$, which the two graphs share. We confirmed this by running 2-FWL on
both to stabilisation.

\begin{proposition}[Counting paths helps]\label{prop:count}
\SPC{} refines \SPD{}, strictly.
\end{proposition}
Refinement is immediate since $(d,\#\mathrm{sp})$ determines $d$. For strictness we searched
exhaustively over all graphs on $n\le6$ nodes: a witness exists at $n=6$ and none at $n\le5$, so
$6$ is minimal. The two graphs (graph6 \texttt{EFz?} and \texttt{ElN?}, edge lists in
Appendix~\ref{app:expr}) have $6$ nodes, $8$ edges, the same degree sequence and, crucially, the
\emph{same} multiset of pairwise distances $\{1^{16},2^{12},3^{2}\}$, differing only in how many
shortest paths realise them. Hop distance alone therefore provably cannot separate them, which
is exactly what the extra channel is for.

Together these place the method as
$\WL\subsetneq\TRPE\subseteq\text{GD-WL}\subseteq\text{3-WL}$
\citep{zhang2023biconnectivity}.

\paragraph{Expressive power is not enough: injection matters.}
The propositions above concern what the \emph{encoding} can distinguish, and say nothing about
whether an architecture can read it.

\begin{proposition}[Normalised aggregation cancels edge weighting]\label{prop:cancel}
Let the node features be uniform, let the rewired edge weights depend only on $d(u,v)$, and let
every node have the same weighted degree $D$ --- as holds whenever $G$ is vertex-transitive, but
constant weighted degree is the operative hypothesis. Then a GCN layer using those weights, or a
GAT layer using them as attention biases, computes an output independent of the weights.
\end{proposition}

The proof (Appendix~\ref{app:proof}) turns on both architectures normalising their aggregation to
sum to one. This is not a corner case: CSL, the benchmark that most sharply motivates positional
encodings, is vertex-transitive with uniform features, so a weight-only model \emph{cannot}
exceed chance there, and in our experiments it scored $10.00\pm0.00\%$ across 20 runs. The
weighting reaches the layer only through the scalar $D=\sum_k n_k w(k)$, which normalisation
divides out, whereas $p_v=\sum_k n_k(v)\gamma(k)$ is vector-valued and, the $\gamma(k)$ being
linearly independent, retains the whole profile $\{n_k\}$: a sum is not renormalised, which is
why that channel survives. The literature reports relative encodings almost exclusively
inside dense attention, where the question does not arise; carrying one into a sparse,
normalising architecture makes \emph{how} it is injected as consequential as what it encodes.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Datasets.}
We use three benchmarks from \citet{dwivedi2023benchmarking}. \textbf{CSL} (150 graphs, 41
nodes) is $4$-regular and featureless; 1-WL sees ten identical classes, so a plain GNN can only
guess at $10\%$. We follow the stratified $5$-fold cross-validation protocol
\citet{dwivedi2023benchmarking} specify for it. \textbf{CLUSTER} (12k graphs, $\sim$117 nodes,
node classification) is a six-community stochastic block model with average degree $\approx37$
and diameter $2$--$3$; exactly one node per community carries its class label and every other
node is featureless, so the task is to propagate six seeds outwards. \textbf{CIFAR10-SP} (45k
graphs, $\sim$117 nodes) is sparse, long-diameter $8$-nearest-neighbour superpixel graphs; every
condition, controls included, discards its edge weights, so all models see an unweighted graph
with the superpixel coordinate as a node feature. The last two are the dense and sparse arms of a
deliberate contrast, a relative distance encoding carrying information only if distances spread
out. The distance profiles we measure before training
(Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostic}) qualify both arms, though: at $r=2$ CLUSTER's $E_r$ is nearly
complete, the $3.3\times$ edge blow-up being saturation rather than growth, while the same radius
reaches only $22.8\%$ of CIFAR10-SP's pairs at mean diameter $8.5$. The dense arm is tested with
its distance structure exhausted and the sparse arm with most of it truncated away.

\paragraph{Conditions.}
\textbf{M0} is the architecture with no PE (control); \textbf{M1} adds Laplacian eigenvector
PEs with sign-flip augmentation; \textbf{M2} adds random-walk PEs; \textbf{M3} is \TRPE{}; and
\textbf{M4} adds the shortest-path-count channel. Each runs under both GCN and GAT.

\paragraph{A prediction registered before training.}
The distance profiles of the CSL classes are fixed by the dataset, so the ceiling our encoding
can reach there is computable in advance. At $r=3$ only $6$ of the $10$ classes have distinct
profiles; at $r\geq4$, $9$ do. The two that remain tied are CSL$(41,6)$ and CSL$(41,16)$, whose
distance multisets are identical, and adding shortest-path counts separates them. We therefore
predict, before running anything, a ceiling of $\mathbf{90\%}$ for M3 and $\mathbf{100\%}$ for M4
on CSL, and we set $r=6$ (its diameter). For CLUSTER and CIFAR10-SP we use $r=2$.

The same diagnostic predicts \emph{where} the encoding can help at all: mean diameter $2.2$ on
CLUSTER against $8.5$ for CIFAR10-SP and $6.0$ for CSL, so if the method behaves as claimed it
should give little or nothing on CLUSTER and help on the other two.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
Four message-passing layers, hidden width $64$, residual connections, batch normalisation, Adam
at $10^{-3}$. Model selection is on validation accuracy and the test set is read once, at the
selected epoch, rather than averaged over the final epochs. We do not match parameter counts across conditions, and the overhead is
not negligible: on an $\sim$18k-parameter backbone the PE modules add $12$--$14\%$ under GCN and
$35$--$37\%$ under GAT, so we do not claim capacity is equalised. The \textbf{rewire-only}
ablation, which runs M3's modules at $19{,}975$ parameters against its $20{,}215$, settles the
question directly (Table~\ref{tab:abl}).

\paragraph{Ablations.}
On CLUSTER with GCN --- and, we should say up front, nowhere else --- we vary the radius
$r\in\{1,2,3\}$, the encoder, and the injection depth (Appendix~\ref{app:abl}). Two variants are
load-bearing. \textbf{rewire-only} is the rewired graph with a \emph{constant} edge feature ---
all of M3's connectivity, none of its positional signal --- and the only condition that attributes
an improvement to the encoding rather than the rewiring it requires.
\textbf{weight-only} and \textbf{profile-only} isolate the two channels of \S\ref{sec:method},
which Proposition~\ref{prop:cancel} makes vacuous and essential respectively on CSL but says
nothing about on CLUSTER, where features are genuine.

\paragraph{Expressivity.}
Table~\ref{tab:expr} (Appendix~\ref{app:expr}) confirms each proposition on its witness. On CSL,
M0 is pinned at exactly $10.00\pm0.00$ in \emph{both} architectures: chance, zero variance
over 20 runs, as the 1-WL bound requires on featureless $4$-regular graphs. M3 reaches $85.50$
under both backbones, agreeing to the decimal, and M4 reaches $99.50$/$100.00$. That agreement
is not a coincidence but Proposition~\ref{prop:cancel} as a measurement: on CSL the weight
channel cancels under both architectures, so each model is reduced to a function of the same
profile $p_v$, and backbone-independence is what the proposition predicts. The ceilings pre-registered in
\S\ref{sec:setup} hold: M4 meets its $\mathbf{100\%}$ and M3 approaches its $\mathbf{90\%}$ from
$4.5$ points below, so the prediction bounds the outcome without fixing it. Combinatorially, \SPD{} separates $97.8\%$ of the non-isomorphic
1-WL-colliding CSL pairs and \SPC{} all of them, the $144$ \SPD{} misses being precisely the
CSL$(41,6)$/CSL$(41,16)$ block: Proposition~\ref{prop:count} has an instance on a standard
benchmark. M1 and M2 also solve CSL and M3 loses to both: the ceiling working rather than the
method failing, since \SPD{} cannot exceed $90\%$ here while LapPE and RWPE are not bound by
it.

\paragraph{The registered prediction failed.}
\S\ref{sec:setup} committed in advance to the encoding helping where distances are spread out
(CIFAR10-SP, mean diameter $8.5$) and giving little on CLUSTER, at $2.2$. \textbf{The opposite
happened.} Our largest gain anywhere is on CLUSTER (GAT M4 at $69.85$ against an M0 control
of $48.57$, $+21.3$ points, exceeding LapPE by $4.9$), while CIFAR10-SP, the predicted best
case, barely moved: $+2.5$ and $+2.1$ under GCN (M3, M4), $+0.5$ and $-0.1$ under GAT. That
control is itself unstable across its three seeds ($\pm11.74$ against $\pm0.22$ for M4) and
$21.3$ is under $2\sigma$ of that spread, so we read the sign, not the magnitude. We report the miss rather than
reframe around it, and Table~\ref{tab:expr} offers an explanation:
neither CLUSTER nor CIFAR10-SP produces a \emph{single} 1-WL collision among the sampled pairs,
so expressivity was never the binding constraint and an intervention justified by it had no
route to helping. That diagnostic is graph-level, though, and so supports the CIFAR10-SP half
only; CLUSTER is node classification, where the binding quantity is within-graph node-colour
collisions, which we did not measure. The prediction was not unlucky, it was aimed at the wrong
bottleneck.

\paragraph{Injection, not sparsity, decides the outcome.}
Holding dataset and encoding fixed and changing only the backbone, CLUSTER moves $-2.2$ and
$-0.9$ under GCN (M3, M4) but $+18.0$ and $+21.3$ under GAT, a $20$-point swing from the
backbone alone. The contrast is not that GCN can never read the encoding --- on CIFAR10-SP it gains
$+2.5$ and $+2.1$, its best cell in that single-seed column --- but that on the one dataset where
the encoding has something large to give, it gives only to GAT, which is
Proposition~\ref{prop:cancel}'s mechanism outside the setting where we could prove it. GAT admits
the encoding as an additive attention-logit bias, a genuine edge-feature channel; GCN has none,
so it is compressed into a scalar gate on an aggregation that is then renormalised. CLUSTER being
neither vertex-transitive nor uniformly featured, the mechanism degrades the signal rather than
annihilating it. The ablation agrees: under GCN \textbf{weight-only} scores $38.84$ against
\textbf{profile-only} at $20.79$, so the channel that survives here is the one that provably
cancels on CSL, and the channel that rescued CSL is actively harmful.

Two things bound that cell. At $r=2$ CLUSTER's rewired graph is nearly complete, so GAT with M3
or M4 is structurally close to a small dense graph transformer with a shortest-path attention
bias, and its gain over a $4$-layer \emph{sparse} baseline cannot be separated from that
architectural change. And the level is unremarkable absolutely: $69.85$ is about where a plain
$16$-layer GAT sits in \citet{dwivedi2023benchmarking} ($70.59$ at $528$k parameters), while our
M0 ($48.57$) sits well below their $4$-layer GAT ($57.73$), so the $+21.3$ is internal to this
setup rather than an absolute gain.

\paragraph{Attribution.}
Table~\ref{tab:abl} and Figure~\ref{fig:abl} (Appendix~\ref{app:abl}) report the ablation, all
ten variants at 40 epochs and one seed against the grid's 60 and three, so they compare only to
each other ($r=2$ scores $36.11$ here against M3's $37.67$ in Table~\ref{tab:main}). Every one is
GCN/CLUSTER: nothing here controls the GAT cell above.

T-RPE at $r=2$ exceeds \textbf{rewire-only} (the same rewired graph, constant edge feature) by
$15.5$ points at $1.2\%$ fewer parameters, so the gap is not a capacity artefact --- but
\textbf{rewire-only} is the wrong control to stop at. The ablation also contains $r=1$, the
original graph with a constant encoding (\S\ref{sec:method}), at $35.20$: rewiring with no
positional signal costs $14.6$ points against not rewiring at all, and $r=2$ recovers only $+0.9$
over $r=1$. Almost all of the $15.5$ points is therefore T-RPE repairing damage the rewiring did
to admit it, and Table~\ref{tab:main} agrees at its own budget, GCN/CLUSTER M3 sitting $2.2$
points \emph{below} M0: the encoding does real work, mostly undoing its own precondition. Three variants beat the pre-registered configuration we report as M3 ---
\textbf{layerwise} injection ($39.57$), a \textbf{one-hot} encoder ($39.34$),
\textbf{weight-only} ($38.84$) against its $36.11$ --- but one-hot outscoring sinusoidal is not
evidence against \S\ref{sec:method}'s smoothness argument: at $r=2$ there are two distances and
nothing to interpolate, so the ablation cannot test that argument at this radius.

\paragraph{Cost.}
Preprocessing is cheaper than a Laplacian decomposition but is not the real cost
(Appendix~\ref{app:cost}): rewiring multiplies the edge count by $2.9$--$6.0\times$, on every
epoch. The radius buys nothing \emph{on CLUSTER} ($r=3$ scores $30.29$ against $36.11$), the only
dataset we swept it on (\S7).

\section{Limitations and Conclusion}

Three limitations of the method are structural: connected strongly regular graphs are exactly what the method
cannot handle (Proposition~\ref{prop:upper}); it has little to say on dense, short-diameter
graphs; and the radius costs edges, $2.9$--$6.0\times$ of them, for an accuracy benefit we never
measured. The last is weaker than it looks, since the radius sweep ran only on CLUSTER, whose
diameter of $2.2$ means larger $r$ provably adds nothing, while \S\ref{sec:setup}'s
pre-registration says the opposite where distances do spread ($r=3$ separates six CSL classes,
$r\ge4$ nine, which is why CSL runs at $r=6$).

The rest are ours, and they land on the headline cell. Our M0 controls sit below published
figures on CLUSTER and CIFAR10-SP, so comparisons here should be read between conditions and not
against the literature. CLUSTER is three seeds and the $+21.3$ is under $2\sigma$ of the
control's own spread; every ablation is GCN/CLUSTER, so that cell has no rewiring or
single-channel control; and its rewired graph at $r=2$ is nearly complete, making the cell an
architectural change as much as an encoding. CIFAR10-SP ran at a single seed \emph{and} at $r=2$,
which reaches $22.8\%$ of its pairs, so the long-diameter arm was under-tested as well as wrong.

One tension is ours to own. \S\ref{sec:intro} divides encodings into absolute (per node) and
relative (per pair) and we set out to defend the relative side, but the channel that works is
$p_v$, a per-node vector and therefore \emph{absolute} by that taxonomy, while the genuinely
relative channel is the inert one. The relative signal became usable only once projected back to
per-node, which is a result about the taxonomy as much as about the method.

Our clearest results are negative, and we report them as findings: an encoding injected as an
edge weight or attention bias is provably inert under normalised aggregation
(Proposition~\ref{prop:cancel}) and empirically scores chance, and our pre-registered prediction
was wrong in the opposite direction because it assumed expressivity was the binding constraint on
benchmarks where we could find no 1-WL collisions at all. What survives is one positive claim,
about injection rather than encoding: the same signal worth nothing under GCN is worth $+21$
points under GAT. The useful question is not which structural signal to compute, but which
architectures can read one at all.

\section{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:cancel}}
\label{app:proof}
Write $w_{uv}=w(d(u,v))$ for the edge weight, $n_k(u)$ for the number of nodes at distance $k$
from $u$, and $\hat d_u=\sum_v w_{uv}$ for the weighted degree. Then
$\hat d_u=\sum_{k=1}^{r} n_k(u)\,w(k)$, so the weighted degree is determined by $u$'s multiset of
incident distances. That multiset is the same at every node when $G$ is vertex-transitive, since
an automorphism carrying $u$ to $u'$ is a distance-preserving bijection of $V$; hence
$\hat d_u=\sum_k n_k w(k)=D$ for all $u$, which is the hypothesis of the proposition and the only
use made of vertex-transitivity.

GCN's symmetric normalisation then gives
$\hat w_{uv}=w_{uv}/\sqrt{\hat d_u\hat d_v}=w_{uv}/D$, so
$\sum_u \hat w_{uv}=\hat d_v/D=1$ for every $v$. With $h_u=h$ for all $u$, the layer returns
$\sum_u \hat w_{uv}Wh_u=Wh$, independent of $w$. For GAT, uniform features make every attention
logit at $v$ equal up to its bias $b(d(u,v))$, and the softmax renormalises those to coefficients
$\alpha_{uv}$ summing to one; the layer returns $\sum_u\alpha_{uv}Wh_u=Wh$ for any bias, this
time without needing constant weighted degree. In both cases the output is a convex combination
of identical vectors, so the weighting survives only through the scalar $D$, which normalisation
divides out. $\square$
